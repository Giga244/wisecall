# Studying in Poland: how to get in and what to know before you go

For Georgian students, Poland is often the first European option: it’s close, many programmes are taught in English, living costs are
often lower than in Western Europe, and after graduating you may get access to the EU labour market. This guide walks you through where to
start and what to watch out for.

> Fees, deadlines and requirements change every year and differ by university. Always check the official pages below and the specific
> university’s website.

## 1. Choose a programme and a university

Poland has public and private universities. At public universities, studying in Polish is often free for Polish citizens and some
foreigners; English-taught programmes usually charge tuition. Private universities almost always charge fees.

Search for programmes on the “Study in Poland” portal run by the Polish National Agency for Academic Exchange (**NAWA**). Compare:

- the language of instruction and the fee;
- accreditation of the programme and the university’s reputation in your field;
- the city — Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, Gdańsk and others differ in living costs and job opportunities;
- chances for internships during your studies.

## 2. Find out whether your diploma must be recognised

For a bachelor’s programme, your Georgian school certificate usually has to be recognised in Poland. Sometimes the university does this
itself; sometimes you need a separate procedure (nostrification) or an apostille on your diploma. For a master’s, you need your Georgian
bachelor’s diploma and transcript. Ask the university’s Admissions Office exactly what you need — and keep the answer in writing.

## 3. Language

- **English-taught programme:** usually IELTS, TOEFL or another certificate; some universities test your English in an online interview.
- **Polish-taught programme:** proof of Polish is required; some universities offer a one-year preparatory course.

Basic Polish helps even on an English-taught programme: daily life, renting a flat and finding a part-time job become much easier.

## 4. Documents and the application

A typical list (check the specific programme):

1. School certificate or diploma, transcript, and where required an apostille and a sworn translation;
2. Language certificate;
3. Copy of your passport;
4. Photos;
5. Motivation letter or CV (for some programmes);
6. Receipt for the application fee.

You usually apply through the university’s online system. Deadlines vary, but for international students they are often earlier because the
visa takes time. Once admitted, you receive an acceptance letter, which you need for the visa.

## 5. Money and scholarships

Plan for two main costs: **tuition** (if any) and **living** — a flat or dormitory, food, transport and insurance. For the visa you usually
have to show that you can cover living costs — check the exact requirement on the Polish consulate’s page.

Scholarships: NAWA programmes, university discounts and scholarships, and Erasmus+ exchanges (if you already study at a Georgian university).

## 6. Visa and residence

With your acceptance letter, book a student (national, type D) visa at the Polish embassy or consulate. After you arrive, if your studies
last longer than the visa, you must apply for a temporary residence permit at the voivode’s office. This takes time, so start as soon as
you’re allowed to.

## 7. Studying and working

Students usually have the right to work alongside their studies, but the rules depend on your status and they change. Before starting a
job, check what your visa or residence permit allows. And don’t lose your studies: many students work too much in their first year and fail
exams.

## 8. Your first weeks in Poland

Many small tasks await you when you arrive, so it helps to know them in advance:

- **University registration** — the student card (legitymacja) gives student discounts on transport and other services; check the exact
  conditions locally.
- **Housing** — dormitory (akademik) places are often limited, so apply early. When renting privately, always sign a written contract and
  read the deposit terms.
- **Insurance** — you need insurance for the visa and the residence permit; find out which policy is valid and what it covers.
- **Bank and phone** — a local account and number make daily life and starting a job easier.
- **The Georgian community** — big cities have Georgian students and communities; they often give the best tips on housing and practical
  matters, but always check official rules in an official source.

## How much to plan for

We deliberately don’t give exact numbers because they vary a lot by city, university and year. Build your budget this way: take the
programme’s annual fee from the university website, add housing in that city (the official dormitory price or rental listings), food,
transport, insurance, visa and residence fees, translation and apostille, and tickets. Then compare it with the amount the consulate asks you
to show for the visa. It’s wise to have a few months of reserve too, because finding a job takes time.

## Rough timeline

| When (before your studies start) | What to do |
|---|---|
| About 12 months before | Choose a field and 3–6 universities, check language requirements |
| 9–6 months before | Language test, apostille and translation of documents, applications |
| 6–3 months before | Acceptance letter, visa appointment, look for housing |
| Final months | Visa, insurance, ticket, plan the residence-permit process |

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing only by price** — a cheap private programme may be poorly regarded in your field. Check accreditation and graduates’ experience.
- **Believing every promise an agent makes** — anyone who “guarantees” admission and a visa is a warning sign.
- **Postponing the residence permit** — the procedure is long and your visa may expire.
- **Ignoring the language** — even on an English-taught programme, Polish makes daily life much easier.
