# Your first job in IT: how to start without experience

“You need experience to get a job, and a job to get experience” — most IT beginners know this loop. The good news is that in IT you can
often build experience before your first job: through projects, internships, open source and small freelance tasks. This guide walks you,
step by step, towards your first offer.

> This article gives general advice. The market changes fast — get information about specific companies and salaries from current job
> ads and from people who work in the field.

## 1. Pick one direction

“IT” is a very broad word. At first, pick one direction and focus on it:

- **Frontend development** — the part of websites and apps users see (HTML, CSS, JavaScript and one framework).
- **Backend development** — servers, databases, APIs (for example Node.js, Python, Java, C#, Go).
- **QA / testing** — quality checking; often an easier way in, with a later move into test automation.
- **Data analytics** — SQL, Excel, visualisation, basic statistics.
- **UI/UX design, DevOps, cybersecurity** and more.

How to choose? Watch job ads published in Georgia for a few weeks: which directions have entry-level demand, and which requirements keep
repeating. Then try the basics of each for a week or two — you’ll see what you enjoy.

## 2. Learn systematically

There are many ways to learn: university, paid bootcamps, free online courses and documentation. What matters isn’t where you learn but that
you **use what you learn in projects**.

Practical tips:

1. Make a 3–6-month plan: which topics, in what order, how many hours per week.
2. After each topic, build a small project — don’t just watch videos.
3. Learn Git and GitHub from the start — you’ll need them in every direction.
4. Learn to read official documentation (for example MDN for web technologies). You’ll use this skill every day at work.
5. Improve your English — documentation, searching for errors and many international companies work in English.

Before signing up for a paid course, find out who teaches it and what projects students build, and talk to graduates. Treat promises of a
“guaranteed job” with caution.

## 3. Build a portfolio

You have to show an employer that you **can** do the work. Two to four well-finished projects are much better than ten unfinished ones.

A good project:

- solves a real problem (even a small one — for you, a friend, a local organisation);
- is on GitHub with clean code and a README explaining what it does and how to run it;
- is deployed online if possible, so it’s easy to look at;
- shows that you think about tests, errors and the user.

## 4. CV and LinkedIn

- **One page** is enough for a beginner.
- At the top: the direction you’re looking for and your main skills.
- Projects with links, each with one or two sentences: what you did and with which technology.
- Previous jobs (even outside IT) — highlight skills that carry over: responsibility, communication, problem-solving.
- Adapt your CV slightly for each job ad — use the words from the ad if you really have that skill.

Keep your LinkedIn profile consistent with your CV. Write what you’re learning and building — recruiters often search there.

## 5. Internships and first experience

- **Internships and junior programmes** — many companies open intakes at certain times of year. Follow company pages and job sites.
- **Open source** — even small fixes are real team-work experience.
- **Volunteering and small freelance tasks** — a website for an NGO or a local business.
- **Hackathons and events** — a good way to meet people and work in a team. Events and programmes are often announced on the website of
  the Georgian Innovation and Technology Agency (GITA).

## 6. Interviews

IT interviews usually have several stages: an HR call, technical questions, sometimes a take-home task or live coding. Prepare like this:

- revise the basics of your direction and know why you made each decision in your projects;
- think out loud while solving a task — how you think often matters more than a perfect answer;
- if you don’t know something, say so and show how you would find out;
- prepare questions for the company: how the team works, who will help you in the first months, how learning happens.

After each rejection, ask for feedback if possible. Rejection is a normal part of it — many beginners send a lot of applications before their
first offer.

## A rough plan

| Period | What to do |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Choose a direction, analyse job ads, learning plan, Git |
| Months 2–4 | Systematic learning, 2–3 projects, GitHub profile |
| Months 4–5 | CV, LinkedIn, first applications, internships, events |
| Month 5+ | Interviews, improve from feedback, a new project |

These timings are a guide — everything depends on how much time you give it each week and where you start from.

## Common mistakes

- **Learning everything at once** — three languages and five frameworks at a shallow level help less than one direction done well.
- **“Tutorial hell”** — endless courses without your own projects.
- **Postponing applications “until I’m ready”** — you’ll never feel fully ready; start early and learn from interviews.
- **The same CV everywhere** — small adjustments change results.
