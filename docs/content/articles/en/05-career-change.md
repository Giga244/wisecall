# Changing careers at 25, 30 or 40: how to move into a new field

If going to work every Monday feels heavy, or you feel you’ve stopped growing in your field, thinking about a career change is normal. Many
people change direction at every age. The key is to do it deliberately — so that you don’t get hurt financially or emotionally. This guide
shows how to plan the change step by step, with less risk.

> There are no salary figures or “most in-demand jobs” lists here — they change fast. Get that information from current job ads, official
> statistics (Geostat) and people who work in the field.

## 1. Understand what you’re leaving and what you’re heading towards

Before you choose a new field, answer honestly:

- What don’t I like now — the work itself, the environment, the manager, the pay or the schedule?
- Could this be solved in the same field, at another company or in another role?
- What kind of work makes me lose track of time? What do people ask me for advice about?
- What matters to me: stability, income, freedom, working with people, creativity?

Sometimes it turns out you don’t need a complete switch but a **change of role in the same field** (for example from sales to marketing, or
from bookkeeping to financial analysis). That’s the lowest-risk route.

## 2. Assess your transferable skills

You don’t start from zero in a new field. Write down the skills that work elsewhere too:

- communication and negotiation;
- project management and organisation;
- working with numbers, analysis, Excel;
- leading a team;
- knowledge of a specific sector — for example a teacher moving into education technology, or a nurse moving into healthcare administration.

Knowledge from your previous field is often an advantage: there’s less competition where two fields meet.

## 3. Test the new field before you leave

The biggest mistake is quitting before you really know the field. Try:

1. **Talk to 3–5 people** who already work in it: what a normal day looks like, what they like and don’t, how they started.
2. **Read 20–30 job ads** — which skills are asked for at entry level, which requirements repeat.
3. **Do a small project** or a short online course — do you like the work itself, not just the idea of it?
4. **Try it part-time or as a freelancer** — even unpaid, for one project.

## 4. Plan your finances

Your income may drop for a while during the switch. Before you decide:

- calculate your essential monthly costs;
- build a financial cushion for several months of expenses — how many depends on your situation;
- discuss it with your family if others depend on your income;
- consider whether you can learn alongside your current job — the safest route, though tiring.

## 5. Learn what you actually need

You don’t always need a new degree. In some fields (parts of IT, design, marketing) a portfolio and practical skills matter more. In others
(medicine, law, psychology, accounting and other regulated professions) formal education and a licence are **required** — in that case, check
the requirements with the relevant ministry and professional body.

## 6. Build your new story

When you send your first applications, the employer should understand why you’re changing fields:

- at the top of your CV, state your new direction and the skills that connect to it;
- describe past experience through results that relate to the new role;
- prepare a short answer to “why are you changing careers?” — positive and forward-looking, without criticising your old job;
- use your network: many first opportunities come through recommendations.

## A rough plan

| Stage | What to do |
|---|---|
| Months 1–2 | Self-assessment, conversations with people in the field, job-ad analysis |
| Months 2–4 | A short course or project, build a financial cushion |
| Months 4–8 | Systematic learning alongside your job, portfolio or certificate, first freelance/volunteer project |
| Month 8+ | Applications, interviews, the move |

The timings are only a guide — moving into a regulated profession takes much longer.

## Dealing with fear and doubt

Almost everyone changing careers has doubts: “it’s too late”, “I’ll have to start from scratch”, “what will people say”. That’s normal. A few
things that help:

- **Break the big decision into small experiments.** One course, one conversation, one project — each step gives you information and costs
  you nothing.
- **Write down the worst case and a plan for it.** If the new field doesn’t work out, can you go back to the old one? Often the answer is
  yes, and that reduces the fear.
- **Find people on the same path.** They give you motivation and practical tips.
- **Track your progress.** Write down monthly what you learned and did — change is slow and easy to miss.

## Common mistakes

- **Quitting impulsively** after a bad day, without a plan.
- **Romanticising the new field** — every job has routine; check whether you’re ready for that routine.
- **An expensive course** before you know the field.
- **Hiding your previous experience** — it’s your advantage, not a weakness.
