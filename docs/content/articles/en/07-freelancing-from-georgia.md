# Freelancing from Georgia: working with foreign clients and paying tax

Freelancing lets you work from Georgia with clients all over the world — in design, programming, translation, marketing, writing and more.
Freedom comes with responsibility: finding clients, setting prices, getting paid and handling tax. This guide covers all of it.

> Tax rules change and depend on your situation. What follows is a general overview — check everything on rs.ge and with an accountant.

## 1. Know what you’re offering

“I do everything” sells badly. Define clearly:

- **Which service** — for example “logo and brand design for small businesses” or “WordPress websites”;
- **For whom** — what kind of client, which country, what size of company;
- **What result** — what the client gets at the end and by when.

The narrower the offer, the easier it is for a client to see why you.

## 2. Portfolio

Clients buy what they can see. If you don’t have clients yet:

- create 3–5 sample projects as if for a real client;
- do one or two projects for someone you know or an NGO in exchange for a review;
- for each piece, briefly describe the task, what you did and what the client got.

## 3. Where to find clients

- **International freelance platforms** — a good start for beginners because clients are already there, but competition is high and
  platforms take a fee. Check fees and rules on each platform’s official page.
- **LinkedIn and professional networks** — posts about your work, direct contact with companies.
- **Referrals** — a happy client is the best sales channel. Ask for a recommendation or a review.
- **Local businesses** — Georgian companies need freelancers too.

## 4. Setting your price

Price isn’t just “what others charge”. Calculate:

1. how much you want to earn a month, including tax and costs (computer, software, internet, insurance);
2. how many hours you can really bill — communication, finding clients and admin take time too;
3. divide the first by the second to get your minimum hourly rate.

Then compare with the market. A **project price** is often better than hourly: the client knows the total in advance, and you earn more by
being efficient.

## 5. Contract and communication

- Agree in writing before starting: scope, deadlines, number of revisions, price, payment schedule;
- On large projects take an **upfront payment** (for example part at the start);
- Keep the correspondence — you’ll need it in a dispute;
- Reply on time — in freelancing, reliability is often worth more than talent.

## 6. Getting paid

Ways to receive money from a foreign client: a bank transfer to your Georgian account (often a separate currency account), or through a
platform or international payment service from which you move money to a Georgian bank. Compare fees and exchange rates — at the bank and on
the service’s official page. Keep invoices and the history of every payment.

## 7. Tax: individual entrepreneur and small-business status

Freelance income is taxable. Many freelancers register as an **individual entrepreneur** and apply for **small-business status**, under which
income up to a certain annual threshold is taxed at a reduced rate. Things to check [VERIFY on rs.ge]:

- the current rate and the annual income threshold above which the status changes;
- which activities are excluded from the status;
- how often declarations are due and the deadlines;
- whether you need to register for VAT (rules for services to foreign clients are different).

You register at the Public Service Hall and request the status on the Revenue Service portal. One consultation with an accountant the first
time saves a lot of time.

## Work routine and rest

The hardest part of freelancing is often not the work but organising yourself:

- **Set working hours** — and tell clients when you’re available. Time-zone differences can easily turn you into a 24-hour operator.
- **Have a workspace** — even a corner of a room, or a coworking space.
- **Track time and income** — you’ll learn which client and which kind of project really pays.
- **Keep a reserve** — freelance income is irregular; a few months of expenses saved lets you work calmly.
- **Keep learning** — your field changes, and the best way to raise your price is a new, in-demand skill.
- **Rest** — nobody will schedule your holiday; plan it like a project.

## How to raise your price

Once you have clients and more work, it’s time to raise prices. Offer new clients the new price from the start; notify existing clients in
advance, for example a month ahead, and explain the value they get. Specialising also raises your price: there are many “designers”, fewer
“designers for fintech apps”. Keep results and reviews — they’re your best argument for your price. And don’t be afraid of a “no”: not every
client is your client. Higher prices often bring better, more respectful clients.

## Common mistakes

- **Very low prices for too long** — attracts difficult clients and leads to burnout.
- **Working without a contract** — especially with a new client.
- **Postponing tax** — accumulated debt and fines are a common freelancer problem.
- **Depending on one client** — if they leave, your income disappears.
