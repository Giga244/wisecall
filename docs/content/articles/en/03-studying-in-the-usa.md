# Studying in the USA: admissions, scholarships and the visa — where to start

An American university is a dream for many, but the process is very different from Europe: there is no single national exam, admission is
holistic (they look at grades, essays, activities and recommendations), and the price can be very high — or, with the right strategy,
much lower thanks to financial aid. This guide covers the main steps.

> Requirements, deadlines and prices change every year. Always check the university’s website, EducationUSA and the US Embassy’s official
> page.

## 1. Start with EducationUSA

**EducationUSA** is the US State Department’s official network that advises international students for free on how to apply to American
universities. It offers advising in Georgia too — see its official page and events. It’s the best first step before you pay an agent.

## 2. Decide what kind of study you want

- **Undergraduate:** usually 4 years; in the first years you take general courses and choose your major later.
- **Graduate (master’s / PhD):** more specialised; PhD programmes often come with funding in exchange for research or teaching
  assistantships.
- **Community college:** 2-year, cheaper programmes, from which you can sometimes transfer to a 4-year university.

## 3. Tests

- **English:** TOEFL, IELTS or another test the university accepts.
- **Standardised tests:** SAT or ACT for undergraduate (many universities are now “test-optional”, others still require them); GRE or GMAT
  for some graduate programmes.

Check each university separately — there is no general rule.

## 4. Building your list

Make a balanced list: a few “realistic” universities, a few “reach” schools and a few “safe” ones. For each, look at:

- financial aid for international students (not every university offers it);
- whether admission is “need-blind” or “need-aware” for international applicants;
- the strength of the programme in your field;
- city, climate and living costs.

## 5. The application: Common App and essays

Many universities accept undergraduate applications through the **Common App**. The main parts:

1. **Personal essay** — your story, what grades don’t show. Not a CV in prose, but one specific experience and what you learned from it.
2. **Supplemental essays** — for a specific university (“why us?”).
3. **Activities** — olympiads, volunteering, work, projects, sport. Quality matters more than quantity.
4. **Recommendations** — teachers who know you well.
5. **Transcript** — your Georgian school grades with an English translation.

For graduate programmes: a Statement of Purpose, CV, recommendations and sometimes a portfolio.

## 6. Financial aid and scholarships

- **University financial aid** — some universities are generous to international students, others give nothing. Always find out in advance.
- **Fulbright** — a well-known government programme, mainly for master’s and research; check the conditions for Georgians on the official
  page.
- **Grants and assistantships** for PhD programmes.

For the visa you must show that you can cover the first year’s costs — this appears on the I-20 form the university issues.

## 7. The F-1 student visa

After admission and receiving your I-20: pay the SEVIS fee, fill in the DS-160 form and attend an interview at the US Embassy in Tbilisi. Be
ready to explain why this programme, how you’ll fund your studies and what your plans are. See all details and current fees on the embassy’s
official page.

## 8. How much to plan for

Prices in the US vary more than anywhere: public vs private universities, state, city and financial aid give the same student completely
different totals. So don’t rely on a general figure. For each university, find its official “Cost of Attendance” (tuition, housing, food,
insurance, books, personal expenses) and subtract the aid they actually offer you. Add what that list doesn’t include: test and application
fees, SEVIS and visa fees, tickets and your first months’ costs on arrival.

Many universities have a “Net Price Calculator”. It isn’t always accurate for international students, but it’s a good starting point.

## 9. Working while you study

Work on F-1 status is limited: mainly on campus and within set hours, and off campus only with special authorisations (for example CPT and
OPT). These rules are strict, and breaking them can cost you your status. So don’t plan your studies around job income, and always ask the
university’s International Student Office before starting any work. The exact rules are on the Study in the States website.

## Rough timeline (undergraduate)

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| ~18–24 months before your studies start | EducationUSA, first version of your list, prepare for English and SAT/ACT |
| ~12–15 months before | Tests, describe your activities, essay ideas, choose recommenders |
| ~9–12 months before | Essays, Common App, applications (Early / Regular deadlines) |
| ~4–6 months before | Decisions, compare financial aid, choose |
| ~2–4 months before | I-20, SEVIS, DS-160, visa interview |

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving money until the end** — admission doesn’t mean free; find out the aid terms from the start.
- **The same essay everywhere** — a generic “why us?” answer works badly.
- **A list that’s too narrow** — only famous universities often ends in all rejections.
- **Handing everything to a paid agent** — the essays and decisions must be yours.
