# How to start a small business in Georgia: from registration to your first customer

Registering a business in Georgia is relatively simple — often it can be done in a day. The hard parts are elsewhere: testing the idea,
choosing the right legal form, understanding taxes and finding your first customers. This guide goes through each step in order.

> Tax rates, thresholds and registration fees change. Check everything on the official pages of the Revenue Service (rs.ge) and the
> Public Registry (napr.gov.ge), and make the decision together with an accountant.

## 1. Test the idea before spending money

Before you rent a space or buy equipment:

- **Talk to potential customers** — not friends, but people who would really pay. Ask how they solve this problem now and how much they pay.
- **Look at competitors** — their prices, reviews, what customers complain about.
- **Sell a small version** — a pre-order, a trial service, one product online. The first payment is the best proof.
- **Do simple numbers** — what one unit costs to make, what you’ll sell it for, how many customers a month you need to cover costs.

## 2. Choose a legal form

The two most common options:

- **Individual entrepreneur (IE)** — simple registration and bookkeeping. An IE can have **small-business status**, under which income up to
  a certain threshold is taxed at a reduced rate. Check the exact rate, threshold and excluded activities on rs.ge [VERIFY]. An IE is liable
  for obligations with their personal assets.
- **Limited liability company (LLC)** — a separate legal entity; partners generally aren’t liable with their personal assets. More convenient
  with partners, or when raising investment or a grant. Under the Estonian model, profit tax is mainly charged when profit is distributed
  [VERIFY the rate and rules on rs.ge].

The right choice depends on income, costs, partners and plans. Decide it **with an accountant** — one consultation costs far less than fixing
a mistake later.

## 3. Registration

You register with the National Agency of Public Registry — at any Public Service Hall branch or online. You’ll need ID, a legal address, a
description of the activity, and for an LLC a charter (a standard template exists). After registration you receive an identification code
and are automatically registered as a taxpayer.

See the current fees and timelines on napr.gov.ge and psh.gov.ge.

## 4. Taxes — what to know from the start

- **VAT** — if your taxable transactions exceed the set threshold over any continuous 12-month period, VAT registration is mandatory. Check
  the threshold and rate on rs.ge [VERIFY].
- **Income tax** withheld from employees’ salaries and **pension contributions** — if you hire someone.
- **Declarations** — monthly or annual depending on your status. Missing a deadline means a fine.
- **The Revenue Service portal** (rs.ge) — where you file declarations, request status and receive notices.

It’s wise to have an accountant from the first year, even part-time or outsourced.

## 5. Bank, payments and permits

- Open a **business account** — keep personal and business money separate.
- If you take payments online, sign an **internet acquiring** contract with a bank; for in-person sales, a POS terminal.
- Some activities need a **licence or permit** (for example food, medical services, financial services). Check with the relevant agency
  whether this applies to you.
- Put contracts with customers and suppliers in writing.

## 6. State support

Georgia has state programmes for entrepreneurs — for example **Produce in Georgia** (Enterprise Georgia) and, for startups, **GITA** grants.
Conditions, co-financing and deadlines change often, so check the current terms on their official websites. Be prepared: almost everywhere
you’ll need a business plan and a budget.

## 7. Your first customers

- start with your network — acquaintances, former colleagues, local communities;
- set up a simple online presence: a Google Business Profile, a social media page and, if needed, a website;
- ask your first happy customers for reviews;
- don’t price too low at the start — count all your costs and your time.

## A simple financial plan

A bank, a grant provider or just you yourself should see the numbers. A simple spreadsheet is enough to start:

1. **One-off costs** — registration, equipment, website, first stock, renovation.
2. **Monthly costs** — rent, utilities, salaries, accountant, marketing, software, bank fees.
3. **Revenue** — how many units you’ll sell a month and at what price, after taxes. Start with a cautious assumption.
4. **Break-even point** — how many sales a month you need to cover monthly costs.
5. **Cash flow** — how much money you need until revenue exceeds costs. Many businesses close in this period, not because the idea is bad
   but because the money ran out too early.

Update the spreadsheet monthly with real numbers — you’ll quickly see where your assumptions were wrong.

## When you need a lawyer

You can do a lot yourself, but a lawyer is worth it when you open an LLC with partners (shares, decision-making, exit rules), sign a large
lease or supplier contract, start a licensed activity, or process personal data (for example in an online shop). A one-off consultation is
often much cheaper than resolving a dispute later.

## Common mistakes

- **A big investment before testing the idea.**
- **Choosing between IE and LLC without an accountant.**
- **Missing the VAT threshold** — registering late after crossing it leads to fines.
- **Mixing personal and business money.**
