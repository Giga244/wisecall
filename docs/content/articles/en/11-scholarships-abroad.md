# Scholarships to study abroad: how to find them and win one

Studying abroad is expensive, but many Georgian students study on a full or partial scholarship. A scholarship isn’t “luck” — it’s often the
result of an application that was well planned, started on time and written carefully. This guide covers the main types of scholarships,
well-known programmes and how to strengthen your application.

> Scholarship conditions, deadlines and eligible countries change every year. Always check the conditions of the programmes below on their
> official pages.

## 1. Types of scholarships

- **Government scholarships** — programmes run by the host country’s government (for example Chevening — UK, Fulbright — USA, DAAD —
  Germany). They often cover all costs and value leadership and ties between countries.
- **EU programmes** — Erasmus+ (exchange semesters from Georgian universities and joint master’s programmes — Erasmus Mundus).
- **University scholarships** — a discount or full funding, often automatic based on your application, or through a separate competition.
- **Foundations and companies** — focused on a specific field or group.
- **PhD funding** — often like a job: a research or teaching assistantship with a salary.

## 2. Well-known programmes for Georgians

- **Erasmus+** — if you study at a Georgian university, find out which European universities it has exchange agreements with. For joint
  master’s programmes (Erasmus Mundus) you apply directly. Information in Georgia: the Erasmus+ National Office website.
- **Chevening** — the UK government’s master’s scholarship; usually requires work experience. Check current requirements and the deadline on
  chevening.org.
- **Fulbright** — a US government programme for master’s, research and more; announced in Georgia through the US Embassy.
- **DAAD** — the German Academic Exchange Service, with several scholarship types (master’s, PhD, short research stays). Each has its own
  requirements.

Some countries (for example Japan, South Korea, Turkey, the Czech Republic, Hungary and others) also offer government scholarships —
information is on that country’s embassy website [VERIFY whether Georgians are eligible this year].

## 3. How to find them

1. Start with **field and country** — what you want to study and where.
2. Look at the **university’s page** for “scholarships” or “funding” — often the most realistic source.
3. Subscribe to updates from official programmes and **embassy** websites.
4. Make a **spreadsheet**: programme, what it covers, requirements, deadline, documents needed, status.

## 4. What assessors look for

Scholarships usually assess more than grades:

- **A clear goal** — why this programme, why now, what you’ll do after;
- **A link to your experience** — what you’ve already done in this direction;
- **Impact** — how you’ll use your knowledge in Georgia or your field (many government programmes value this highly);
- **Leadership and initiative** — with specific examples, not general words.

## 5. The motivation letter

- start with a specific story or problem, not “I’ve dreamed of this since childhood”;
- each paragraph answers one question: what you did, what you learned, why this programme, what your plan is;
- tailor it to **each programme** — a generic letter shows;
- check the word limit and answer every question;
- give it to two people to read — one who knows the field and one who doesn’t.

## 6. Recommendations and language

- ask recommenders early — at least several weeks ahead — and give them your CV, a draft of your letter and the deadline;
- choose someone who knows you **specifically**, not the person with the highest title;
- take your language test (IELTS, TOEFL, German and others) early — results take time and you may need to retake it.

## The scholarship interview

Many government scholarships hold an interview at the final stage. Prepare like this:

- **Know your application** — the interview is often built on your letter; you should be able to talk in detail about every sentence.
- **Prepare short answers** to: why this programme, why this country, what your plan is after your studies, how you’ll use your knowledge in
  Georgia.
- **Be specific** — numbers and examples from your experience work better than general statements.
- **Practise out loud** — with a friend or a mentor, in English or the programme’s language if possible.
- **Know the programme’s values** — some value leadership and networking, others academic research.

## Rough timeline

| When (before your studies start) | What to do |
|---|---|
| 15–18 months before | Choose field and countries, scholarship spreadsheet, language preparation |
| 12–15 months before | Language test, choose recommenders, first drafts of your letter |
| 9–12 months before | Many government scholarship deadlines fall in this period — check each one |
| 3–6 months before | Interviews, results, visa |

## If you don’t win this year

Rejection is common — the competition is strong. Ask for feedback if the programme gives it, review your letter and application, strengthen
your weak points (language, work experience, projects) and try again next year. Many scholars win on their second or third attempt.

## Common mistakes

- **Finding out about the deadline too late** — many scholarships close more than a year before studies start.
- **The same letter everywhere.**
- **Relying on one scholarship** — have several options.
- **Paid “scholarship-winning” services** — applying for an official scholarship is normally free.
